Friends, let me tell you about the first time I ever saw Loose Sausage. I was seven years old, standing on a wobbly step stool in my Grandma Darlene's kitchen in the middle of a Kentucky ice storm, and she was hunched over a cast iron skillet that she swore was older than the state highway. The power had flickered twice, the radio was playing polka for reasons nobody could explain, and she looked down at me and said, "Honey, some sausage wants to be a patty, and some sausage wants to be free." I didn't understand it then. I'm not sure I fully understand it now. But I've been chasing that smell of sage and black pepper ever since.

Fast forward about thirty years and I've got three kids, a husband named Gary who believes the dishwasher loads itself, and a golden retriever named Biscuit who, yes, is named after the thing I serve this on. Our Saturday mornings are pure chaos. Somebody can't find a shoe, somebody else has decided they're a vegetarian until lunch, and Biscuit is usually eating a sock. But the second that pork hits the pan, every single one of them shows up in the kitchen like I rang a dinner bell. My middle son Tanner calls it "the crumbly stuff," which I've decided to take as a compliment.

I have to tell you a quick story about my kitchen, because it matters. Last spring we finally redid the backsplash (subway tile, very brave of us), and the contractor, a lovely man named Ron, told me I'd need to "baby" the grout for a few weeks. Well, Ron, I made Loose Sausage on day two. I'm sorry. I couldn't help it. There's a little speck of fennel-flecked grease up behind the stove that I now think of as a family heirloom. Gary wants me to scrub it off. Gary is not in charge of the backsplash.

The real turning point for this recipe was our road trip to Asheville two summers ago. We stopped at a tiny diner where the waitress had a beehive hairdo and a name tag that just said "MA'AM." I ordered biscuits and gravy, and the sausage in that gravy was so beautifully loose and crispy at the edges that I teared up a little, right there in the booth. Tanner asked if I was okay. I said, "I'm better than okay, baby, I'm inspired." I spent the whole drive home scribbling notes on the back of a gas station receipt about nutmeg and a little pinch of brown sugar, while the kids fought over who got to hold the bag of boiled peanuts.

So this is my version, the one I've tinkered with through holidays and hangovers, snow days and soccer tournaments. It's what I make on Christmas morning while everyone is still in pajamas tearing into stockings, and it's what I make on a random Tuesday when the week has gotten away from me and I need something warm that tastes like Grandma Darlene's kitchen. It's humble, it's honest, and it doesn't pretend to be anything it isn't. Some sausage wants to be a patty. This sausage wants to be free, and I think we should let it.

## Ingredients
- 1 lb (450 g) ground pork, about 20% fat
- 1 tsp kosher salt
- 1 tsp dried sage
- 1/2 tsp dried thyme
- 1/2 tsp freshly ground black pepper
- 1/2 tsp fennel seeds, lightly crushed
- 1/4 tsp red pepper flakes
- 1/4 tsp ground nutmeg
- 1 tsp brown sugar
- 1 clove garlic, finely grated
- 2 tbsp ice-cold water
- 1 tsp neutral oil

## Instructions
1. In a small bowl, mix the salt, sage, thyme, black pepper, fennel, red pepper flakes, nutmeg and brown sugar.
2. Put the ground pork in a large bowl. Add the spice mix, garlic and ice-cold water, then work it with your hands for about 1 minute, until the meat feels a little sticky and the seasoning is spread evenly. Don't shape it into links or patties, and don't use casings. It stays loose.
3. Chill the mixture for at least 30 minutes, or up to overnight, so the flavors can blend.
4. Heat the oil in a large skillet over medium-high heat until it shimmers.
5. Add the sausage and press it into an even layer. Leave it alone for 2 to 3 minutes so the bottom browns well.
6. Break it into small, uneven crumbles with a wooden spoon or potato masher. Keep stirring and cook for another 5 to 7 minutes, until no pink is left, the edges are crisp, and the inside reaches 160°F (71°C).
7. Drain off extra fat if you like, taste, and add a pinch more salt if it needs it.
8. Serve the loose sausage right away over eggs, grits or biscuits, or in a pan gravy. Leftovers keep in the fridge for up to 4 days.
